# English Journey: A2–C2 Curriculum Expansion Plan

**Prepared for:** English Journey

**Audience:** Arabic-speaking learners, while retaining an English-first course experience for any learner.

**Status:** Proposal only — no A2–C2 content will be added until you approve this plan.

## 1. The curriculum decision

The expanded course should not become six larger word lists. It should become a sequence of **increasingly independent ways of using English**. CEFR is designed around what learners can do with language, rather than a prescribed list of grammar points or words. Oxford similarly positions its CEFR-aligned word and phrase resources as guidance for curriculum design, while Cambridge presents the framework as a step-by-step path across language skills.[1] [2] [3]

> **The design principle:** A learner does not “finish” a word when they see its translation. They grow from recognising it, to using it in a sentence, to combining it naturally with other words, to choosing it precisely for an audience and purpose.

Therefore, every level will combine vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, reading, listening-ready transcript work, writing, and AI feedback around **situations, ideas, and outcomes**. New vocabulary will be varied by theme, communicative purpose, frequency, word family, and collocation — **never alphabetically**.

| Course range | Learner transformation | Course language | Primary evidence of growth |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A2** | From surviving everyday exchanges to managing familiar life | Bilingual English/Arabic, with frequent contrast notes | A short connected description, message, and routine conversation |
| **B1** | From familiar sentences to connected opinions and stories | English first; Arabic used for difficult contrasts | A coherent personal story, practical email, and supported opinion |
| **B2** | From communicating to persuading, explaining, and discussing | Mostly English; Arabic on request | A balanced argument, extended discussion, and complex-text analysis |
| **C1** | From competent use to flexible academic/professional use | English-first; Arabic only as a targeted support tool | A structured essay/proposal and interpretation of implied meaning |
| **C2** | From fluent use to precise synthesis, tone control, and rhetorical choice | English immersion; Arabic glossary only | A multi-source synthesis, nuanced critique, and audience-aware writing |

Oxford’s guidance identifies A2 as immediate-relevance communication, B1 as connected text and reasons for opinions, B2 as complex text and explained viewpoints, C1 as implicit meaning and flexible academic/professional use, and C2 as precise synthesis across sources.[1] Those outcomes become the level gates in English Journey.

## 2. Exponential growth: the learning architecture

The growth should be **cumulative, not merely additive**. At every new level, a learner meets three types of language:

| Layer | Share of a lesson’s active language | Purpose | Example progression |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Retrieve** | 20–30% | Bring earlier language back into memory | A1 *go, yesterday, friend* → B1 “I went with a friend yesterday because…” |
| **Extend** | 45–55% | Add level-appropriate words, grammar, chunks, and pronunciation | B1 *reliable, look forward to, although* |
| **Transform** | 20–30% | Reuse known language for a new function, register, or audience | B2 turn a personal complaint into a formal email requesting a resolution |

This is the **Language Spiral**. A known item is deliberately revisited at least four times: as recognition, guided production, free production, and later-level transformation. The platform will make this visible with a “Previously learned” label, for example:

> **A1:** “I like this restaurant.”  
> **A2:** “I like this restaurant because the food is inexpensive.”  
> **B1:** “I would recommend this restaurant because it offers good value for money.”  
> **B2:** “Although the service was slow, the restaurant offers excellent value overall.”  
> **C1:** “Despite occasional delays in service, the restaurant’s value proposition remains compelling.”

This pattern makes advancement tangible. It also prevents the common problem of introducing advanced vocabulary without strengthening the grammar, phrase patterns, and confidence needed to use it.

## 3. Scope and sequence

The A1 course remains the foundation: 20 lessons, 500 high-frequency words, core grammar, pronunciation, and simple output. A2–C2 will retain a modular course map, with each module ending in a performance check. The proposed content counts below are **authoring budgets**, not claims about what a learner must memorise. They will be validated against CEFR-aligned Oxford/Cambridge reference resources, but all lesson wording, examples, passages, and tasks will be newly authored.

| Level | Suggested structure | New language focus | Reading & writing progression | Capstone performance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A2** | 20 lessons; 4 modules of 5 | Approx. 500 high-value lexical targets, plus chunks and basic collocations | 120–180-word notices, posts, messages, and simple narratives; 60–100-word writing | Plan an event, describe an experience, and solve an everyday problem |
| **B1** | 24 lessons; 4 modules of 6 | Approx. 450 word families; phrasal verbs, common collocations, opinion frames | 250–400-word blogs, stories, reviews, and practical articles; 130–180-word writing | Tell a coherent story and defend a practical choice |
| **B2** | 24 lessons; 4 modules of 6 | Approx. 400 lexical networks; discourse markers, register, idioms used carefully | 450–650-word articles and talks; 180–260-word article, review, or argument | Research a current issue and present a balanced case |
| **C1** | 24 lessons; 4 modules of 6 | Approx. 350 precise lexical networks; academic/professional collocations, stance language | 700–900-word essays, features, and reports; 280–350-word essay/proposal | Produce a well-structured, evidence-aware argument |
| **C2** | 20 seminars; 4 modules of 5 | Approx. 250 nuance networks; rhetoric, style, rare-but-useful precision | 900–1,400-word multi-genre dossiers; 400–600-word synthesis/critique | Compare sources, detect assumptions, and write for a defined audience |

Oxford’s resources underline why later levels should be organised by more than headwords: the Oxford Phrase List includes idioms, phrasal verbs, collocations, and prepositional phrases, and the English Vocabulary Profile distinguishes meanings and phrases, not just individual words.[4] [5] Thus a B2 lesson on *change* may cover **shift, transform, adapt, lead to, result in, in response to, gradual, abrupt**, and the difference between neutral, formal, and persuasive wording.

### A2 — Everyday independence

| Module | Big question | Lesson themes | Language result |
|---|---|---|---|
| A2.1 **My life in motion** | How do I describe routines, habits, and change? | routines, health, free time, comparison, frequency | Present/past links, comparatives, adverbs of frequency, everyday collocations |
| A2.2 **People and places** | How do I explain my world to another person? | neighbourhood, travel, services, culture, directions | descriptive language, there is/are expansion, relative clauses in basic form |
| A2.3 **Experiences and plans** | How do I tell someone what happened and what I intend to do? | memories, celebrations, problems, future plans, invitations | past narrative, present perfect introduction, future forms, advice |
| A2.4 **Choices and communication** | How do I make requests, give reasons, and state a simple opinion? | shopping, work/study, technology, media, simple opinions | modals, reason/result, requests, short connected messages |

The A2 end point is not a harder A1 quiz. It is an **Everyday Life Project**: plan a weekend for a visitor, solve two realistic problems, write a message and a short review, then answer follow-up AI questions using target chunks.

### B1 — Connected experience and opinion

| Module | Big question | Reading studio | Writing/speaking outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1.1 **Stories that matter** | How do I make an experience interesting and clear? | personal narratives, news anecdotes | 150-word story with sequencing, detail, and reflection |
| B1.2 **Work, study, and identity** | How do I explain what I want and why? | profiles, advice columns, workplace messages | application email and a spoken self-introduction |
| B1.3 **Travel, change, and challenges** | How do I deal with problems in English? | travel reports, practical notices | complaint/request with facts, tone, and a proposed solution |
| B1.4 **Opinions in public** | How do I agree, disagree, and support a view? | short opinion pieces, interviews | 170-word viewpoint with reasons, examples, and concession |

At B1, each lesson has a **Language Upgrade** section. It shows a serviceable A1/A2 sentence, then lets learners choose a B1 upgrade. The AI tutor should explain not only correctness, but the effect: *“Your first version is clear; this version sounds more connected because ‘although’ signals contrast.”*

### B2 — Explanation, debate, and register

| Module | Big question | Deep-language focus | Performance task |
|---|---|---|---|
| B2.1 **Ideas and evidence** | How do I distinguish a claim, an example, and evidence? | hedging, contrast, cause/effect, reporting verbs | explain an issue using evidence without overclaiming |
| B2.2 **Science, work, and technology** | How do I explain a complex process to different audiences? | nominalisation, passive choices, technical-but-clear wording | turn a technical summary into a public explanation |
| B2.3 **Society and choices** | How do I make a balanced case? | concession, counterargument, conditionals, persuasive frames | moderated written debate |
| B2.4 **Culture and communication** | How does language change with audience? | formality, humour, idiom boundaries, diplomatic language | compare an informal post with a formal recommendation |

The B2 capstone is a **Position File**. The learner reads two short original texts with different views, identifies claims and evidence, then writes a balanced response. This reflects the B2 move toward understanding complex text and explaining advantages and disadvantages of options.[1]

### C1 — Analysis, precision, and professional control

| Module | Big question | Deep-language focus | Performance task |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1.1 **Reading between the lines** | What is implied rather than stated? | stance, presupposition, implicature, evaluative language | annotate implicit meanings and justify the interpretation |
| C1.2 **Academic and professional argument** | How do I structure a persuasive, fair argument? | cohesion, synthesis, cautious claims, evidence integration | 300-word argument or proposal |
| C1.3 **Systems, ethics, and policy** | How do I discuss complex choices responsibly? | qualification, counterargument, precision, institutional language | policy-style briefing with options and risks |
| C1.4 **Voice and audience** | How do I sound natural in different contexts? | register shift, concise editing, rhetorical emphasis | rewrite one idea for a manager, public audience, and peer |

At C1, Arabic is no longer the default teaching language. The learner sees an English explanation first, then may open **Arabic contrast** when a structural difference is genuinely useful. This preserves accessibility without stopping English immersion.

### C2 — Synthesis, style, and subtle meaning

| Seminar | Challenge | Required skill |
|---|---|---|
| C2.1 **Nuance and judgement** | Decide how strongly a claim can be made | calibration, modality, precise qualifiers |
| C2.2 **Argument across sources** | Reconcile disagreement between two authors | synthesis, source attribution, evidence weighting |
| C2.3 **Style under pressure** | Change tone without changing meaning | rhetorical control, concision, idiomatic choice |
| C2.4 **The expert reader** | Read challenging texts critically | ambiguity, rhetorical strategy, assumptions, irony |

The C2 capstone is a **Synthesis Portfolio**. Learners work with a set of newly authored or openly licensed texts, map the arguments, write a concise synthesis, then revise it for a different audience. This targets the C2 ability to synthesise multiple sources and express fine shades of meaning.[1]

## 4. The learner-facing lesson plan

Every lesson should tell the learner exactly what to do and why. The current “Words / Grammar / Speak / Read / Write / Quiz” tab model becomes a clearer **six-step path**, while retaining the individual tools.

| Step | Learner sees | What happens | Time guide |
|---|---|---|---:|
| **1. Start here** | “By the end, you can…” and a 90-second warm-up | Retrieve 3–5 earlier items; see how today builds on a previous lesson | 5 min |
| **2. Explore** | A short story, dilemma, dialogue, or scenario | Meet target language in meaningful context before definitions | 8–12 min |
| **3. Notice** | “How English works here” | Grammar, phrase patterns, pronunciation, register, and Arabic contrast where helpful | 10–15 min |
| **4. Build** | Guided choices and sentence upgrades | Use new language with feedback and meaningful constraints | 10–15 min |
| **5. Respond** | Read/listen-ready text, speaking prompt, or writing task | Produce a personal, practical, or analytical answer | 10–20 min |
| **6. Prove it** | Mastery check and next step | Level-appropriate quiz plus adaptive review scheduling | 5–10 min |

This makes a typical A2 lesson approximately **45–60 minutes**, B1/B2 approximately **60–75 minutes**, and C1/C2 approximately **75–100 minutes**. Cambridge’s indicative guided-hour totals should be treated as a planning reference, not a guarantee: study time changes with intensity, background, and exposure.[6]

### What makes advanced lessons engaging

Higher levels should feel like **English is now the medium of interesting thought**, not a subject made of disconnected exercises. Each module will include a recurring “studio” activity:

| Level | Studio | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A2 | **Life Lab** | plan a day, solve a travel problem, compare local options |
| B1 | **Story & Choice Lab** | narrate an event, choose a solution, explain the reason |
| B2 | **Debate Lab** | build a claim, react to an opposing argument, revise tone |
| C1 | **Analysis Lab** | identify what a writer implies, critique evidence, write a brief |
| C2 | **Synthesis Lab** | connect several sources, choose a stance, edit for a specialist audience |

The AI should not simply announce a score. It should act as a coach with a fixed feedback order: **meaning first, organisation second, language choice third, accuracy fourth, then one prioritised revision task**. For higher levels, it should challenge unsupported claims and ask targeted follow-up questions rather than merely correcting grammar.

## 5. Content standards and data model changes

Each new content item should be original and carry sufficient metadata for progression, re-use, and AI control.

| Content type | Required fields | Quality rule |
|---|---|---|
| Lexical network | headword/phrase, CEFR target, meaning, word family, collocations, register, pronunciation, Arabic support, previous-level links | Teach a usable pattern, not only a translation |
| Grammar/expression | can-do outcome, meaning, form, pronunciation, discourse effect, Arabic contrast, common error, controlled-to-free practice | Explain *why a speaker chooses it* |
| Reading text | level, genre, target/retrieval language, estimated time, source/license, questions by reading skill | New/or openly licensed material; no copying from Oxford/Cambridge resources |
| Writing task | audience, purpose, genre, length range, success criteria, language constraints, AI rubric | Rubric changes by level; not one generic grammar score |
| Pronunciation target | IPA, stress, linked-speech feature, minimal-pair/contrast, recording prompt | Move from isolated sounds to intelligibility and delivery |
| Assessment item | skill, difficulty, linked targets, distractor rationale, retry/review rule | Test transfer and use, not only recognition |

Oxford and Cambridge resources should serve as **reference points for CEFR alignment**, vocabulary meaning, phrase type, and British/American variation. We will not copy their copyrighted word-list entries, dictionary definitions, exercises, or passages into the product. English Journey will author its own explanations, tasks, example sentences, and original or properly licensed reading materials.

## 6. Build order after approval

The project should expand **one validated level at a time**, because the lesson design will become better after feedback from the six-person pilot.

| Release | Build scope | Learner outcome | Approval gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Release 1: A2** | 20 lessons, content pipeline v2, language spiral, Life Lab, revised lesson path | Everyday independence | Pilot A1 feedback and A2 content/editorial sign-off |
| **Release 2: B1** | 24 lessons, Story & Choice Lab, richer writing rubric, phrase/collocation engine | Connected stories and opinions | A2 completion data and quality review |
| **Release 3: B2** | 24 lessons, Debate Lab, source-aware reading, register practice | Complex explanation and balanced argument | B1 quality/engagement review |
| **Release 4: C1** | 24 lessons, Analysis Lab, English-first scaffolding, advanced writing feedback | Academic/professional flexibility | B2 evidence of readiness |
| **Release 5: C2** | 20 seminars, Synthesis Lab, precision/rhetoric engine, capstone portfolio | Independent high-level analysis and expression | External educator review before public claims of C2 readiness |

## 7. Recommendation and decisions needed

I recommend beginning with **A2 next**, not attempting all remaining levels simultaneously. It delivers the first meaningful proof of exponential growth: A1 learners see familiar language return in richer sentences, longer texts, and practical outcomes. The A2 pilot should also validate the improved six-step lesson path before B1–C2 are authored.

Before implementation, please confirm three choices:

1. Should A2 launch with the proposed **20 lessons / four modules**, or would you prefer a more intensive 24-lesson course?
2. Should we add **learner audio recording and AI pronunciation feedback** in the A2 release, or retain browser playback first and schedule feedback for B1?
3. For higher-level reading, do you prefer mostly **original English Journey articles** or a carefully curated mix of original and openly licensed authentic texts?
