# C1 Module 5 Architecture Packet

## Module identity

**Working title:** Public Reasoning and Responsible Proposal

**Lesson range:** 65–80

**Level:** C1

**Dominant learner journey:** The learner moves from interpreting contested public evidence to producing a concise, source-aware formal proposal. The module treats advanced English as purposeful decision-making: learners must distinguish observation from inference, qualify claims, compare source reliability, synthesise competing accounts, and communicate a defensible recommendation to a mixed audience.

## Architectural premise

This module does not turn sixteen lessons into a repeated reading–writing sequence. Instead, it varies the dominant experience while preserving a coherent rhetorical arc. Early lessons establish the language of evidence and attribution; the middle lessons make learners interrogate certainty, audience, and trade-offs; the final lessons ask them to synthesise sources and produce a formal proposal with integrity.

> **One lesson, one centre of gravity:** each lesson has one primary objective and one short bilingual retrieval check. Supporting skills appear only when they serve that objective.

## Source-grounded arc

| Lessons | Learner movement | Dominant archetypes | Main evidence produced |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65–68 | From reported observation to calibrated interpretation | Notice, Reading, Grammar, Listening | Annotated claim, attribution choices, confidence scale |
| 69–72 | From source comparison to responsible stance | Interaction, Speaking, Reading, Vocabulary | Source comparison, spoken qualification, audience adjustment |
| 73–76 | From contested evidence to practical decision-making | Real World, Writing, Listening, Review | Trade-off map, formal recommendation, retrieval synthesis |
| 77–80 | From epistemic humility to public proposal | Grammar/Notice, Speaking, Writing, Assessment | Layered hedging, stakeholder briefing, final source-aware proposal |

## Lesson architecture

| Lesson | Primary objective | Archetype | Skill emphasis | Retrieval focus |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| 65 | Separate an observable claim from an interpretation. | Notice | Reading-to-language noticing | Classify a new statement as observation or inference. |

| 72 | Choose register and emphasis for a mixed stakeholder audience. | Vocabulary | Collocation and register | Replace one overconfident phrase with an audience-appropriate alternative. |
| 73 | Map trade-offs before recommending a public action. | Real World | Decision reasoning | State the strongest trade-off in a new scenario. |
| 74 | Write a formal recommendation that distinguishes evidence from judgement. | Writing | Source-aware formal writing | Mark one sentence as evidence and one as judgement. |

## Cadence controls

Reading appears at Lessons 66, 71, and 76, with source interpretation increasing in complexity rather than being mechanically repeated. Writing appears at Lessons 74, 78, and 80, moving from evidence/judgement separation to a formal proposal and then a cumulative transfer task. Listening appears at Lessons 68 and 75 with transcripts disclosed progressively rather than presented as the first view. Speaking and interaction are distributed across Lessons 69, 70, 72, 73, and 79, while grammar is deliberately separated between Lessons 67 and 77. Lesson 76 functions as a dedicated retrieval/review point, and Lesson 80 is the module assessment.

## Bilingual and compact-lesson controls

Every lesson presents an English objective with an Arabic counterpart and an English-first task explanation supported by concise Arabic clarification. Retrieval checks contain both English and Arabic prompts, and their expected response is short enough to expose memory without creating a second assignment. Arabic is used to clarify rhetorical distinctions such as *claim*, *evidence*, *inference*, *qualification*, *trade-off*, and *attribution*; it does not replace the C1 English performance target.

## Progressive disclosure and visual route decisions

The learner’s first view should show the lesson purpose, the single action, and what counts as a useful response. Source excerpts, model answers, transcript text, and detailed language notes remain collapsed until needed. Listening lessons begin with audio controls and a short listening purpose; the transcript is disclosed after the first attempt. Reading lessons foreground the source and one interpretive question before revealing annotation guidance. Writing lessons reveal the planning scaffold before the full model. Assessment feedback separates evidence, language control, and transfer so a learner can revise one dimension at a time.

## Assessment arc

Lessons 65–79 generate small evidence artefacts: classifications, attribution rewrites, source comparisons, spoken qualifications, trade-off maps, and short recommendations. Lesson 76 checks retrieval across the first three quarters of the module. Lesson 80 requires a formal proposal that synthesises a policy extract and critical commentary, makes one defensible recommendation, attributes evidence precisely, acknowledges uncertainty, and identifies a stakeholder trade-off. The assessment should reward intellectual accuracy and communicative control rather than maximal length.

## Quality risks and mitigations

The principal risk is that advanced source work becomes an abstract vocabulary list. The mitigation is to anchor each language choice in a consequential communicative decision. A second risk is over-hedging, where caution becomes evasive. The module therefore contrasts calibrated uncertainty with empty vagueness and requires a clear recommendation in the final tasks. A third risk is source overload; progressive disclosure and one dominant operation per lesson keep the cognitive load appropriate for C1.

## Completion criteria

The module is ready for integration when all sixteen lessons have a typed activity record, an explicit bilingual retrieval check, a distinct first-view route, and a source-grounded objective. At least two listening routes must disclose transcripts progressively, at least three writing routes must culminate in the formal proposal arc, and the final assessment must require synthesis rather than isolated item recall.
