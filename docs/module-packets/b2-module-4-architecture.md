# B2 Module 4 — Research and Curriculum-Architecture Packet

> **Status:** Architecture approved for authoring. The content creator may now produce the Module 4 corpus, but may not replace these can-do outcomes, safeguards, or purpose-led routes without returning the change to the Curriculum Architect.

## 1. Module purpose

This fifteen-lesson B2 module moves the learner from an **individual workplace concern** to a defensible public recommendation. Its through-line is: work and wellbeing → heritage and shared resources → verifying contested information → institutional responsibility → interpreting data → ethical and inclusive public choices → negotiated compromise. The learner is repeatedly asked to make a claim proportionate to evidence, register, audience, and possible consequences.

The authoritative B2 draft sets the content scope: work, purpose, and pressure; heritage and development; public libraries; contested health-policy claims; convenience; workplace culture; institutional failure; statistics; ethical dilemmas; access; representation; reform; risk; expertise; and compromise. This rearchitecture preserves the scope while deliberately avoiding a repeated “article → explanation → formal letter” path. The module’s enduring outcome is not agreement with any proposal; it is the ability to **explain a fair, qualified choice and state what would change that choice**.

### Sensitive-content boundary

| Area | Required content decision | Learner safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Work, burnout, and wellbeing | Treat workload and meaningful work as fictional workplace-policy questions, not personal health diagnosis or employment advice. | Learners can choose a supplied fictional role; no disclosure of work status or wellbeing is required. |
| Health-related claims and risk | Teach verification, calibrated certainty, and source boundaries—not clinical advice or a judgement about real policies. | Clearly label all case materials as fictional learning scenarios and reject certainty unsupported by the supplied text. |
| Heritage, access, and representation | Frame disagreements as competing legitimate interests and design barriers rather than claims about groups. | Accept more than one evidence-grounded position and include accessible language support for high-stakes public terminology. |
| Institutional failure and blame | Analyse attribution choices and remedies without asking learners to accuse real people or institutions. | Use anonymous fictional institutions; distinguish reported claims from established facts. |
| Negotiation and ethics | Make trade-offs visible and require a review condition. | Do not score a learner for preferred values; score clarity, fairness, evidence, and conditional reasoning. |

## 3. Architecture decisions

The learning stages are **not layouts**. A light grammar lesson may move directly from a small contrast to controlled use; a deep public-choice lesson needs evidence and a review condition. Module 4 therefore uses three intentionally narrow focus lessons, seven normal lessons, and five deep transfer tasks. Deep lessons are separated by lower-load routes so that extended civic vocabulary and production do not cluster.

### Compact lesson rule and programme cadence

Every lesson has **one primary objective** and one short retrieval/check. The check may be a single contrast, classification, source decision, spoken response, or sentence completion; it is not permission to add a second full lesson. Secondary skills appear only when they are necessary evidence for the primary objective. A lesson must not automatically contain vocabulary, grammar, reading, writing, listening, speaking, interaction, and assessment together.

The architect audits the previous approximately ten lessons, the current module, and the next objectives before selecting the route. The programme targets are balancing ranges, not a rotation: B2 reading appears about every 1–2 lessons, writing every 3–4, listening frequently and with increasingly authentic input, speaking every 2–3, and interaction or real-world tasks every 2–4 where appropriate. Grammar is spaced rather than placed in consecutive lessons unless progression requires it; dedicated review appears roughly every 8–12 lessons; Lesson 60 is the module assessment. These ranges are checked across the surrounding sequence, not forced inside each fifteen-lesson module.

For the Module 3 → Module 4 boundary, Lessons 36–45 already contain speaking, writing, real-world, reading, integration, notice, interaction, review, and assessment routes. Module 4 therefore deliberately opens with a compact grammar repair, places reading at Lessons 49 and 53, writing at Lessons 48 and 55, speaking at Lesson 54, and inserts a listening route at Lesson 52 before the source-and-stance review. This adds listening without turning the module into a reading → writing → speaking → listening rotation. Lessons 46, 50, 52, 58, and 59 remain deliberately lighter so the surrounding deep tasks are sustainable.

The same layering principle applies across levels: early A1 may introduce a short, highly supported reading or writing route around Lesson 5 using familiar language, while later levels increase independence and authenticity. Increasing difficulty must not mean increasing the number of activity types packed into a single lesson.

## 6. Assessment map

| Assessment point | Evidence sampled | Feedback focus | Fairness rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lesson 48 public-response revision | Position, concession, source-bound evidence, register | Whether the letter distinguishes assertion from support and revises for audience. | Credit more than one conclusion when it engages the supplied evidence and counterpoint. |
| Lesson 49 verification order | Source type, certainty, missing information | Whether the learner explains what a source can establish and what must be checked. | Do not ask learners to judge real health claims or identify their own beliefs. |
| Lesson 53 statistic critique | Headline, data source, hedge, limitation | Whether the learner avoids an overclaim and identifies a relevant limit. | Score reasoning from the fictional text, not statistical expertise beyond the task. |
| Lesson 55 inclusive-design proposal | Measures, constraint, mitigation, formal register | Whether access measures are specific, cost-aware, and understandable. | Assess clarity and response to constraints; do not assume a single ideal design. |
| Lesson 57 reform sequence | Claim, evidence, concession, rebuttal | Whether the learner represents an objection fairly before responding. | A reasoned defence of either option can pass if it is evidence-bound. |
| Lesson 60 compromise position paper | Two options, conditional compromise, counterargument, contingency | Connection between evidence, trade-off, language control, and review plan. | Never score which stakeholders or policy choice the learner favours; score fair and qualified reasoning. |

## 7. Content-creator guardrails

1. Use British English spelling and pronunciation reference by default.
2. Do not use a repeated mentor opening, dialogue frame, question format, or activity sequence merely because the topic changes.
3. Use only valid lesson progression stages, visual semantics, and progressive supports from the shared contract. For this module, use `arabic-help`, `worked-example`, `word-support`, `tip`, `transcript`, or `accessible-tip` only.
4. Treat the draft’s case studies as transparent fictional learning scenarios. Do not fabricate quotations, statistics, testimonials, professional credentials, authorities, or citations as real evidence.
5. Do not make a learner disclose a medical condition, workplace status, political view, heritage identity, or personal experience to complete an activity.
6. Prefer a precise omitted skill to filler. A lesson need not contain reading, writing, listening, and speaking to be complete.
7. First view must answer **What is this? What do I do? What matters? What comes next?** Optional Arabic clarification, examples, transcripts, and worked models must remain intentionally revealable rather than visually dominant.
