# C1 Module 6 Architecture — Evidence, Expertise, and Public Judgment

## Purpose

C1 Module 6 develops a learner’s ability to convert specialist evidence into a responsible public recommendation. The module treats expertise as useful but bounded: learners compare sources, identify what a claim does and does not establish, recognise omitted assumptions, and communicate a defensible course of action without manufacturing certainty.

Each lesson has one primary objective and a short bilingual retrieval check. Activities are deliberately varied rather than rotated. Reading and listening expose learners to source hierarchy, expert qualification, and consequence language; interaction and speaking require fair reformulation and concise public briefing; writing culminates in an audience-sensitive formal proposal.

## Learner journey

| Lessons | Dominant route | Primary learning move | Evidence of learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 81–83 | Encounter, notice, listening | distinguish expertise, evidence weight, and qualification | identify the claim, its basis, and its uncertainty |
| 84–87 | Reading, grammar, interaction, speaking | compare expert claims, hedge responsibly, and challenge assumptions | source comparison, calibrated response, and oral clarification |
| 88–91 | Listening, reading, writing, review | track omission, attribution, consequences, and counterargument | evidence map, formal paragraph, and retrieval transfer |
| 92–95 | Listening, interaction, reading, synthesis | negotiate public meaning while preserving dissent and scope | fair reformulation, paired-source judgement, and recommendation plan |
| 96 | Assessment | make specialist complexity readable for a defined public audience | formal proposal plus concise spoken brief |

## Cadence decisions

The module uses reading in Lessons 81, 84, 86, 89, 93, and 95–96; writing in Lessons 87, 90, 94, and 96; listening in Lessons 83, 88, and 92; speaking in Lessons 85 and 96; and interaction in Lessons 86 and 93. This exceeds the C1 minimum without turning the sequence into a fixed rotation. Grammar is embedded in lessons on hedging, concessive comparison, attribution, and proportionality rather than taught as consecutive isolated units.

A retrieval check appears in every authored record in English and Arabic. The checks ask learners to produce or distinguish one transferable move, such as separating a finding from an inference, qualifying a claim, naming an omitted assumption, or stating a review condition. Transcript-like listening support is hidden behind progressive disclosure in the workspace; the first view presents the task and purpose, while the spoken line or transcript support appears only when requested.

## Assessment arc

Lessons 90 and 94 function as meaningful-use evidence points: learners produce formal writing that can be reviewed for scope, attribution, uncertainty, audience fit, and consequence awareness. Lesson 96 is the module checkpoint. It requires a 300–360-word formal recommendation and a 90-second spoken summary. The assessment is not a vocabulary recall test; it evaluates whether the learner can synthesise source material, acknowledge a limit, propose proportionate safeguards, and make the recommendation readable for a public decision-maker.

## Bilingual and visual decisions

English titles, objectives, prompts, retrieval checks, and support lines are paired with Arabic translations. Semantic labels and icons remain visible so colour is never the only activity cue. The calm first view shows **What is this?**, **What do I do?**, and **What matters?**; source details, model phrasing, and transcript support remain progressively disclosed. The module uses source and evidence motifs without adding decorative density.

## Quality risks and controls

The main risk is that advanced content encourages confident paraphrase without evidential discipline. Every reading and writing route therefore requires explicit attribution or scope control. A second risk is overloading a C1 learner with several output demands at once. Each lesson has one dominant output; the final checkpoint combines writing and speaking only because the architecture packet identifies synthesis as the module-level evidence. A third risk is repetitive formal-proposal practice. The sequence varies the audience, source relationship, and interactional pressure: expert briefing, oversight committee, public consultation, paired-source dossier, and municipal proposal.
