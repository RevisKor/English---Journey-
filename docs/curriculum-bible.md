# English Journey Curriculum Bible

**Status:** Version 0.1 — production foundation

## 1. Purpose and non-negotiable course philosophy

English Journey is a **bilingual Arabic–English, British-English-first course** that develops usable communicative ability from A1 to C2. It is designed as one coherent course rather than a large collection of independent pages. Every lesson must earn its place by moving the learner towards a practical capability, deliberately reconnecting to prior learning, or providing meaningful assessment evidence.

The learner experience should be **predictable in navigation and varied in activity**. A learner should understand where they are, what they are trying to do, and what happens next without encountering a mechanically repeated lesson template.

The curriculum uses the CEFR action-oriented view of language. Its learning outcomes must connect to communicative activity—not only vocabulary or grammar coverage—and should progressively address reception, production, interaction, mediation, and linguistic, sociolinguistic, and pragmatic competence [^cefr].

## 2. Learning-design rules

| Rule | Required application in English Journey | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Small, purposeful increments** | Limit each lesson’s genuinely new material; state one clear can-do outcome. | Novices are more likely to retain learning when new information is manageable and clearly bounded [^aero]. |
| **Scaffold, then fade** | A1–A2 use models, Arabic clarification when needed, worked examples, cues, and constrained practice. B1–C2 reduce these supports and increase independent choice. | Guidance supports novices; gradually fading it promotes independence [^aero]. |
| **Meaningful retrieval** | Reintroduce prior vocabulary, grammar, and communicative routines after a delay in a new relevant context. | Active retrieval improves long-term retention and transfer more effectively than extra re-reading alone [^retrieval]. |
| **Varied practice** | Revisit the same target through recognition, spoken rehearsal, contextual reading, controlled production, and independent use. | Varied retrieval creates more useful future access routes [^aero]. |
| **Assessment fit** | Match the assessment form to the ability: objective items for objective knowledge; structured prompts and rubrics for writing and speaking. | Validity, reliability, fairness, and fitness for purpose are core assessment requirements [^cambridge]. |
| **Arabic as scaffolding** | Use Arabic for difficult instructions, contrasts, and predictable Arabic-speaker errors; do not translate every line. Fade support across levels. | The learner retains English as the working language while receiving targeted support. |

## 3. CEFR progression profile

| Level | Learner capability focus | Support and language policy | Typical evidence of learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Immediate needs, familiar people, objects, routines, short exchanges. | Highly guided; concise English; targeted Arabic explanations. | Identify, name, describe simply, answer and ask predictable questions. |
| **A2** | Everyday transactions, familiar past and future events, short connected texts. | Guided but increasingly English-led. | Handle routine exchanges, describe experiences, write simple connected messages. |
| **B1** | Independent everyday communication and supported opinions. | English-first with targeted contrastive support. | Narrate, explain a viewpoint, understand and create connected texts. |
| **B2** | Complex interaction, register awareness, sustained reasoning. | Limited scaffolding; more authentic sources. | Defend a position, infer meaning, write structured arguments and reports. |
| **C1** | Flexible, precise, context-sensitive communication. | Learner-directed support; nuanced feedback. | Synthesise sources, manage formal/informal registers, express complex positions. |
| **C2** | Highly precise, natural, strategic control. | Minimal scaffolding; demanding authentic tasks. | Mediate complex meaning, adapt style, evaluate and create sophisticated discourse. |

Progression means more than longer texts. It must increase **independence, ambiguity, precision, inference, register awareness, discourse control, and transfer**.

## 4. Curriculum hierarchy and lesson blueprint

The mandatory hierarchy is:

> **Level → Module → Lesson → learning objective → activity sequence → retrieval → assessment evidence**

Every lesson blueprint must record the following before content is authored:

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Identity | CEFR level, module, lesson number, title, estimated duration, dominant experience. |
| Outcome | A learner-facing can-do statement describing an observable ability. |
| Scope | Primary skill, secondary skills, new vocabulary, grammar/discourse targets, and required prerequisites. |
| Connections | Retrieval targets, reinforced concepts, future dependencies, and word-bank source. |
| Experience | Orientation, encounter, noticing, practice, use, retrieval, and evidence stages, adapted rather than repeated mechanically. |
| Difficulty | Cognitive-load estimate, amount of novelty, language complexity, and available scaffold. |
| Quality | Arabic-support decision, British-English check, accessibility notes, and assessment requirements. |

## 5. Lesson families and experience balance

The current application has a foundation of standard, visual-vocabulary, interaction, speaking, writing, reading, review, and assessment lessons. The production system should treat these as **families**, not a rigid repeating cycle, and extend their blueprint metadata to support listening, pronunciation, real-world English, and integrated practice where pedagogically justified.

| Lesson family | Dominant learner experience | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary / visual vocabulary | Discover, classify, recall, and use concrete language. | Visual context genuinely improves meaning. |
| Grammar / language noticing | Notice form–meaning–use, compare, practise, and apply. | A communicative outcome requires a grammatical or discourse tool. |
| Interaction | Respond, clarify, negotiate, role-play, and repair. | The module capability depends on exchange with another person. |
| Speaking / pronunciation | Hear, repeat, contrast, plan, and perform. | Sound, intelligibility, confidence, or spoken routine is central. |
| Reading / listening | Predict, locate, infer, evaluate, and transfer. | Learners need reception skills or real-world comprehension. |
| Writing | Model, plan, produce, revise, and reflect. | Learners need written communication or discourse control. |
| Real-world English | Interpret and use messages, signs, menus, forms, notices, emails, or timetables. | Authentic genre knowledge serves the module outcome. |
| Review / integrated practice | Retrieve, contrast, connect, and use earlier learning in a new context. | A spaced retrieval opportunity is meaningful. |
| Assessment | Demonstrate, not merely recognise, the intended outcome. | There is adequate prior practice and a defined decision purpose. |

## 6. Rhythm, retrieval, and activity guardrails

Lessons should vary between approximately **10–15 minute light**, **15–25 minute normal**, and **25–40 minute deep** experiences. Modules should alternate load, not cluster every intensive skill task together.

The activity library must track recent patterns. A lesson is rejected if it repeats the prior lesson’s dominant experience without a documented pedagogical reason. Retrieval should be contextual and spaced: introduce → recognise → understand → use → retrieve → contrast → independent use.

## 7. Assessment architecture

| Layer | Purpose | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Mini check | Verify immediate objective and correct misconceptions. | 3–5 focused items or a short guided response. |
| Checkpoint | Retrieve recent and earlier learning in mixed contexts. | Approximately 8–15 varied items. |
| Milestone | Measure accumulated mastery and transfer. | Approximately 20–30 items plus suitable production evidence. |
| Module assessment | Test the module’s integrated capability. | Vocabulary, language use, reception, and practical communication as appropriate. |
| Level challenge | Judge whether CEFR-level abilities are credible. | Integrated, level-specific communicative tasks. |

No assessment should test a target solely because it appeared in a particular lesson. The question is: **can the learner use it in a relevant new situation?**

## 8. Bilingual, British English, and accessibility rules

English Journey defaults to contemporary natural **British English**. Explanations, dialogues, and examples must avoid artificial textbook language, forced idioms, stale stock names, and generic motivational filler.

Every essential visual distinction requires **label + icon/shape + accessible colour**, never colour alone. Lessons require readable line lengths, adequate contrast, visible keyboard focus, reduced-motion compatibility, and understandable heading structure. Arabic should be accurate, concise, and contextually helpful; it is support, not a second copy of the course.

## 9. Quality gates

No lesson is complete until it passes the relevant checks below.

| Gate | Minimum decision |
|---|---|
| Research alignment | The design decision is grounded in the evidence register or is explicitly marked as an editorial choice. |
| Curriculum architecture | Outcome, prerequisites, retrieval, module role, and future connection are explicit. |
| Content quality | Language is natural, accurate, British-English-first, purposeful, and non-repetitive. |
| Pedagogical audit | CEFR fit, cognitive load, skill balance, retrieval, Arabic support, and engagement pass. |
| Learning experience | Visual hierarchy, activity recognition, accessibility, and scanability pass. |
| Assessment quality | Assessment form is fit for purpose and can evidence the stated can-do. |
